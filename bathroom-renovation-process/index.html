The bathroom renovation process in Darwin

In short: A bathroom renovation runs from brief and quote to strip-out, rough-in, waterproofing, tiling and handover. In the Northern Territory, prescribed residential building work must be done by a registered building contractor under a written contract, with a fidelity fund certificate before a permit and before any payment, and the standard deposit cap is 5%.

A step-by-step guide with the official rules for your state or territory. It is not legal advice.

Last updated: 24 November 2026

This guide explains a typical bathroom renovation and the official rules that apply in Darwin. It is not legal advice. Follow the links and ask the renovator to confirm what's needed for your home.

1. Brief and budget

Decide what you need: a full renovation, an ensuite, a walk-in shower instead of a bath, or an accessible bathroom. Set a budget with a contingency.

2. Design and quote

The renovator inspects the room, talks through the layout and fittings, and gives a written quote. Check it covers demolition, plumbing, electrical, waterproofing, tiling, fixtures, and rubbish removal.

3. Licences and contract

In the Northern Territory, a building permit is required for some work in building control areas, but not for every renovation. NT Government says renovations or alterations that do not increase the floor area or height, and that do not alter structural elements or the building classification, generally do not require a registered builder. A bathroom renovation on its own usually does not need a fidelity fund certificate, unless it is done with other work that is prescribed. Plumbing and electrical work must still be done by licensed practitioners, and you should ask a registered building certifier whether a permit is needed if you are moving walls or changing structure. Units may also need body corporate approval.

Sources: NT Government, Work that requires permits; Building Practitioners Board; NT Government, Fidelity fund certificate.

In the Northern Territory, prescribed residential building work must be done by a building contractor registered with the Building Practitioners Board, unless you become a registered owner-builder. That includes a new home, and renovations or extensions that are prescribed (including work worth more than $25,000 that increases the residential floor area). A residential building contract is required for prescribed work; under the standard progress-payment schedule the deposit before work starts can be no more than 5% of the contract price. For prescribed work, the builder must also obtain a fidelity fund certificate (the Territory's residential building cover) before a building permit is granted and before demanding payment. You can check a practitioner on the Building Practitioners Board register.

Sources: NT Government, Residential building work that requires a registered builder; NT Government, Signing a residential building contract; NT Government, Fidelity fund certificate; Building Practitioners Board.

4. Strip-out and rough-in

The old bathroom is stripped back, and the plumber and electrician rough in new services. Homes built before about 1990 may contain asbestos, including in wall and floor sheeting in wet areas, which must be handled safely by people qualified to do it.

5. Waterproofing, tiling and fit-off

Waterproofing is applied and allowed to cure before tiling. Then fixtures, screens, the vanity and lighting are installed. See our waterproofing guide.

6. Handover

Inspect the finished bathroom, note any defects in writing, and keep the waterproofing, plumbing and electrical certificates and warranties.

Official pages checked 23 November 2026. Rules can change; check the linked sources before relying on this summary.
